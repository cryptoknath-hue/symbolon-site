Run a service node

Nothing to earn today. Tessera runs on a test chain and the coins are worth nothing. This procedure is worth running because the mechanism needs to be exercised by people other than its author.

A Tessera service node is a Monero node reachable only over Tor, registered on the Tessera chain, which answers availability probes. This page is the whole procedure, end to end.

Read this first: there is nothing to earn today. Tessera runs on a test chain, the coins are worth nothing, and no public network exists yet. What follows is worth doing because the mechanism has to be exercised by people other than its author before it can be taken seriously — not because it pays.

What you are proving, and what you are not

The probe asks your hidden service to sign a value derived from the Tessera chain and from four Monero blocks. A valid signature proves three things at once: the service is up, it holds the private key of the .onion it registered, and it has the Monero blocks the challenge names. Nobody can forge that, not even the auditor who carries the proof — every validator recomputes the expected answer from its own monerod.

What it does not prove is that monerod itself is answering behind that address. The recommended setup below makes the probe responder and the Monero RPC share one hidden service, so the address you register really is your Monero node's address. An auditor may additionally check that monerod answers (wallet.py audit --require-monero), but be clear about the status of that check: the chain cannot verify it. It raises the cost of lying; it is not a rule.

What you need

  • A Monero node you already run, or are willing to run, reachable over Tor.
  • Tor, with permission to create a hidden service.
  • Python 3.12 and the cryptography package.
  • A Tessera node on the same machine, itself talking to an unrestricted monerod RPC.
  • 1 000 TSR of collateral, locked for 10 000 blocks (~7 days) after you deregister.

1. One hidden service, two ports

In your torrc:

HiddenServiceDir /var/lib/tor/tessera/
HiddenServicePort 18081 127.0.0.1:18081
HiddenServicePort 80 127.0.0.1:19083

Port 18081 is your Monero RPC; port 80 is the Tessera probe responder. Both behind the same HiddenServiceDir, so both share one .onion address — which is the point: the address you register is the address that serves Monero.

sudo systemctl restart tor
sudo cat /var/lib/tor/tessera/hostname

That .onion is your node's identity on Tessera.

2. Your Tor key is your identity — treat it that way

/var/lib/tor/tessera/hs_ed25519_secret_key is the private key of your hidden service. Whoever holds it can impersonate your node on Tor and on Tessera. Tessera refuses to work with a key readable beyond its owner:

sudo chmod 600 /var/lib/tor/tessera/hs_ed25519_secret_key

A detail worth knowing, because it explains a Tessera-specific piece of code: Tor does not keep the 32-byte seed. It stores the expanded key, and mainstream ed25519 libraries only sign from a seed. Tessera ships its own ed25519 (tessera/ed25519.py) purely so that an operator can sign with the key Tor actually wrote. It is checked byte for byte against a reference implementation.

3. A wallet, and collateral

python3 tools/wallet.py new          # writes ~/.tessera/wallet.json — NOT encrypted, back it up
python3 tools/wallet.py address
python3 tools/wallet.py balance

Collateral is the only Sybil barrier that exists. It is not a fee: you get it back when you deregister, minus anything a penalty ate.

4. Register

python3 tools/wallet.py register $(sudo cat /var/lib/tor/tessera/hostname) 1000

Consensus accepts only .onion v3 and .b32.i2p endpoints — a clearnet address has no valid form here. One account registers one node; one endpoint can be taken once.

Check it landed:

python3 tools/wallet.py registry

5. Run the responder

python3 tools/service-node.py --tor-key /var/lib/tor/tessera

It does two things: it answers auditors' probes, signing with your Tor key, and it files your own proof of availability each round.

It never signs what it is handed. An auditor sends a round, its address and its nonce; the responder recomputes the expected answer from its own Tessera node, which derives it from the chain, and signs only that. A hostile auditor cannot use your responder as a signing oracle — it does not choose the message, only a nonce that consensus already constrains.

The responder listens on 127.0.0.1:19083. Tor publishes it. Never expose it directly.

As a service, so it comes back after a reboot:

systemd-run --user --unit=tessera-service --collect \
  python3 /path/to/tessera/tools/service-node.py --tor-key /var/lib/tor/tessera

6. Your other job: auditing

Registering makes you an auditor too. Each round the registry is sorted into a ring, and you audit the five nodes after you. Ignoring that is penalised — below 50% of duties fulfilled over an epoch, 5% of your collateral is burnt, never paid to anyone.

python3 tools/wallet.py audit

It probes each node you owe a probe to, over Tor, and files either an attestation or an absence report. Filing an absence counts as a duty fulfilled: an auditor that cannot reach a node has nothing to sign, and the mechanism must not punish it for saying so. A round counts as available with three attestations and at most one absence.

On a timer, since duties change every round:

# ~/.config/systemd/user/tessera-audit.timer — every 10 minutes
[Timer]
OnUnitActiveSec=10min

Be honest in both directions: an absence report contradicted by a strong majority is itself a fault. Crying absence cannot be used to starve a competitor.

Checking your own node

python3 tools/wallet.py availability        # rounds attested, and your own proofs
python3 tools/wallet.py challenge           # what this round asks of you
curl -s http://127.0.0.1:19083/             # the responder, as it presents itself

From another machine, through Tor, the way an auditor sees you:

torsocks curl -s http://<your>.onion/

When it does not work

clé Tor inutilisable : … chmod 600 — the key file is readable by others. Fix the permissions; this one is refusing to help you leak your identity.

n'est pas dans le registre — the responder will not start for an unregistered account. Register first, and wait for the transaction to be in a block.

le registre annonce … la clé donne … — the key in --tor-key is not the key of the endpoint you registered. Two hidden services got mixed up.

le nœud local ne peut pas calculer le défi — your Tessera node has no monerod, or its monerod does not hold the Monero range the challenge names. The challenge draws from the last 1 000 Monero blocks so that a pruned node can still answer; a node syncing from scratch cannot.

Your proof is refused as out of window — a proof is admissible for 60 blocks, a probe for 10. The narrow probe window is deliberate: it is what lets consensus bound when the answer was produced, instead of trusting the auditor's clock.

What this does not protect you from

Registering publishes the association between a Tessera account and a .onion. That is inherent — the chain has to know which endpoint to pay — but it means your node's address and its earnings are linked in public, permanently. Decide whether that matters to you before you register.

~/.tessera/wallet.json holds your private key in the clear. Acceptable while nothing has value; it will not be acceptable on a real network, and it is on the list of things to fix first.
